Executive summary

Although it may seem that elective PSTAT coursework should have a correlation between programming, statistics, and breadth of interest, our various models did not show statistical significance in the correlation between any of these metrics. Programming-related elective coursework, such as CS16 and CS5, did not significantly affect estimates of programming comfortability, nor did it significantly predict advanced programming proficiency. Elective statistics coursework, such as PSTAT 115 and PSTAT 174, was also not an accurate predictor of student comfortability or advanced proficiency in statistics. While investigating the linear correlation between the number of non-mandatory classes taken and the number of areas of interest, we found no significant relationship.

Data description

Data was obtained from a survey offered to all students enrolled in PSTAT 197A. There were 55 total respondents. The data consists of background information on students with columns regarding courses taken, proficiency in math, statistics, and programming. It also contains demographic information on class standing, gender, and race. There is also data on interests in programming languages preferences, domain specification, and technical areas of interest. It is worth noting that at the end of the survey, students were asked to give consent on their information being used in the dataset for analysis. Forty-nine students gave consent to allow their skills, experience, and project preferences to be used in the joint dataset.

Questions of interest

We sought to investigate the relationships between previous courses taken and skill levels and interests. Specifically, we wanted to focus on elective courses and potential effects that they may have on students given that students chose to take said courses. For example, we wondered if taking an additional programming course, such as CS16, would make a student report feeling more comfortable in programming skills.

The specific questions we investigated were:

  1. Can we predict student comfortability/proficiency in statistics using the elective upper-division Statistics courses they have taken?

  2. Is there a linear relationship between reported programming comfortability/ proficiency and elective, programming-related coursework? Can we fit a linear model to it?

  3. Is there a linear relationship between having taken more non-mandatory courses and having more areas of interest?

Question 1

Fitting statistics comfortability and advanced proficiency to a linear model, using PSTAT 115, PSTAT 160, PSTAT 174, and PSTAT 175 as predictors, did not result in an accurate model. In the comfortability model, we achieved an RMSE of about 0.634, which is quite high, and an R-Squared of 0.147. These metrics do not indicate an accurate model. In addition, none of the classes were found to be statistically significant predictors.

In the advanced proficiency model, we achieved a slightly better RMSE of 0.436 and an R-squared of 0.164. Additionally, none of the classes were found to be statistically significant. Again, we could not build a model that accurately predicted advanced statistical proficiency using elective upper-division PSTAT courses.

Question 2

Fitting programming comfortability to a linear model, using CS5, CS9, CS16, PSTAT100, and PSTAT131 as predictors, did not result in a successful model. Across all 5 predictors, each predictor did not have a statistically significant effect on the intercept. This means that for CS5, CS9, CS16, PSTAT100, and PSTAT131, having reported taking that course did not significantly affect what the individual reported as their level of programming comfortability.

Similarly, when fitting programming advanced proficiency in a linear model with the same coursework, each course did not have any statistically significant effect on whether an individual reported advanced proficiency or not. Holding all else constant, PSTAT131, CS5, and CS16 had positive coefficients, indicating that they might have higher predicted odds of advanced proficiency, but still, by looking at the p-value, each course did not have statistically significant effects.

Question 3

What we found in our research is that there is no significant correlation between the number of areas a student is interested in and the number of non-mandatory classes a student takes. Our linear regression model found no statistically significant linear relationship between number of non-mandatory classes taken and the number of areas of interest that the individual marked on the form. Based on the linear regression findings, we got a p-value of 0.364. The effect of non_mandatory indicates that non_mandatory is not a significant predictor of num_areas. Analyzing the residuals of the linear regression model, we can see that the residuals are normally distributed with a mean near zero.
